Camel Trekking in Merzouga: What to Expect

From mounting up at the dune's edge to watching the sun drop over Erg Chebbi — here's the real experience, soreness and all.

3 min readSaharaBy the Shared Morocco Adventures team

If there's one image people picture when they think of a Morocco trip, it's this: a line of camels silhouetted against orange dunes at sunset. It's worth every bit of the hype, but it's also a physical experience most first-timers aren't quite prepared for. Here's what actually happens, step by step.

Mounting Up

Camel treks into Erg Chebbi, the tall dune sea near Merzouga, typically start in the late afternoon from a small staging point where handlers (often called "camel drivers") line up the animals in a train, tied nose to tail. Getting on is the part everyone's nervous about: the camel kneels, you swing a leg over a wooden saddle frame, and then it stands up in two jolting movements — back legs first, which pitches you forward, then front legs, which pitches you back. Hold the front handle, not the reins, and lean back on the first jolt.

Once you're up, the ride itself is a slow, rolling sway, more like a boat than a horse. It's not uncomfortable exactly, but hips and inner thighs will feel it after 45-60 minutes, especially if you're not used to riding.

The Trek Into the Dunes

The walk from the edge of Erg Chebbi to a camp deep in the dunes takes roughly 45 minutes to an hour, moving at a slow, steady walking pace. The landscape changes fast — within ten minutes the road and buildings disappear behind the first ridge of dunes, and it's just sand in every direction, sculpted into ripples by the wind. Wrap a scarf around your head and face during the trek; blowing sand is a real and constant thing here, not just for photos.

💡

Quick Tip

Wear leggings or loose trousers, not shorts — bare skin against the saddle for an hour leads to chafing, and sand gets everywhere regardless.

Sunset Over the Dunes

Timing is everything here, which is why group departures are scheduled the way they are: you leave in time to be dismounted and climbing a high dune on foot as the sun starts dropping, usually around 30-40 minutes before actual sunset. The last stretch is done on foot because loose sand on a steep dune face isn't safe for camels carrying riders. Climbing feels harder than it should — two steps up, one step sliding back — but the view from the top, dunes rolling out gold and pink in every direction, is the payoff for the whole day.

This is consistently the moment travelers on our tours mention first when they get back — not because it's rare exactly, but because experiencing it isn't something you do alone very often. Watching it with a small group, everyone equally stunned into silence, adds something a solo trip doesn't replicate.

Arriving at Camp

By the time you climb back down and remount (or walk, if the camp is close), it's dusk. Desert camps range from simple shared Berber tents to more comfortable setups with proper beds — budget group tours typically use the simpler, more traditional camps, which is honestly part of the appeal. Expect a communal dinner around a fire, often with camp staff playing drums afterward, and a genuinely dark sky overhead once the fire dies down — no light pollution for miles.

What Your Body Will Feel Like

Be honest with yourself about this part: most first-timers are sore in the hips, thighs, and lower back the next morning, and everyone finds sand in bags, shoes, and pockets for days afterward. It's a minor price for the experience. Bring wet wipes, a change of socks, and don't wear your best clothes — this is not a day for anything you're precious about.
