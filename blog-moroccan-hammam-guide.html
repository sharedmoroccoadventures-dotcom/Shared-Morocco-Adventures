Moroccan Hammam Guide: What to Expect

One of the most genuinely relaxing (and least understood) experiences on a Morocco trip.

A hammam is a traditional steam bathhouse, and in Morocco it's less a spa treatment than a everyday ritual — most Moroccans visit a neighborhood hammam weekly, not as a luxury but as routine deep cleaning and a social outing. For travelers, trying one is one of the most authentic cultural experiences available, and far more affordable than the word "spa" suggests.

Local Hammam vs Tourist Spa Hammam

You'll find two very different versions. A neighborhood hammam (hammam beldi) is unglamorous, cheap (often under 20 dirhams for entry), gender-segregated by set hours or separate sections, and entirely self-service unless you pay a small extra fee for a scrub attendant. A riad or spa hammam is set up specifically for tourists, costs considerably more, includes a scrub and often argan oil massage as standard, and is a gentler introduction if the local version feels intimidating for a first visit.

What Actually Happens

You undress to underwear (or fully, depending on the hammam and your comfort) in a changing area, then move through a sequence of increasingly hot steam rooms to open your pores. Once warmed up, you apply savon beldi (a dark, olive-oil-based black soap) all over, let it sit for a few minutes, then either scrub yourself or have an attendant scrub you with a rough exfoliating glove called a kessa — this is the part that removes a genuinely surprising amount of dead skin. A rinse, sometimes a clay mask, and you're done.

💡

Quick Tip

If an attendant scrubs you at a local hammam, a small tip (10–20 dirhams) is customary and expected, even though the base entry fee is already paid.

Etiquette to Know

Hammams are strictly gender-separated, either by alternating hours or separate entrances, so you won't be sharing the space with the opposite sex. Modesty within your own gender's session is relaxed compared to Western norms — most locals go topless or fully nude — but underwear is completely fine and normal for tourists who prefer it. Bring your own flip-flops (floors are wet and can be slippery), and don't expect much English spoken at a local hammam — pointing and smiling gets you a long way.

What to Bring

A local hammam typically expects you to bring your own black soap, kessa glove, shampoo, and a bucket/bowl (all sold cheaply at any nearby hanut or souk stall) — ask your riad if you're unsure where to buy them. A spa hammam usually supplies everything, so just bring yourself and maybe a change of clothes for afterward.

Is It Worth Doing?

Genuinely, yes — even travelers who feel a bit awkward walking in usually leave saying it was a highlight, not just because of how clean and relaxed you feel afterward, but because it's one of the few experiences that isn't staged for tourists. Ask your riad host to recommend a nearby local hammam if you want the authentic version, or add a spa hammam session as an easy half-day treat between longer excursions.
